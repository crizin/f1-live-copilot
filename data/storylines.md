# 2026 Season Storylines

> Things Claude might not know from training data — recent drama, memes, narratives.
> Updated throughout the season as stories develop.
> Championship points live in `standings.md`. This file carries the narrative, not the table.

Last update: 2026-09-14, the morning after the Spanish GP (Round 14, Madrid, September
11-13 — the first race at the new Madring).

**Where it stands:** Antonelli has turned a lead into a stranglehold, and Madrid made it
close to academic. Eight wins now, two on the bounce, and the second of them landed in his
lap: Norris led comfortably until a VSC caught him just past the pit entry and everyone
behind pitted for nearly free. Antonelli said outright that Lando deserved it. Russell is
the closest thing to a rival left and he's 81 points adrift with nine rounds to go.
Ferrari's fortnight has been grim — the two cars collided on lap 1 at Monza and Leclerc
crashed out on lap 2, prompting Hamilton to spend Sunday evening telling anyone with a
microphone that Ferrari has no rules of engagement, then a brake failure ended his Madrid
race inside seven laps. The new circuit itself is the other talking point: a brand-new
track that produced four on-track overtakes all afternoon and a queue of drivers
saying so. The 2027 driver market is largely settled — Sainz to Williams,
Verstappen to Red Bull through 2030 — with Red Bull's second seat the big one still open,
and Hadjar, now three races out with a broken wrist, reportedly in line for an extension.

## The Big Stories

### Zandvoort's last dance — and it ended in the wall

The Dutch Grand Prix is gone from the calendar. The promoter chose not to continue past
2026, citing the long-term financial risk of hosting without state subsidies rather than
any lack of demand; nobody has ruled out a return, but there's no deal. A record 325,000
came across the three days — and Verstappen put it in the barrier at the last corner of
lap one in front of every one of them. Six years of orange, over like that.

### The new rules reshuffled the grid

The 2026 power unit overhaul — electric power tripled, MGU-H gone, 100% sustainable fuel
— redrew the order. Where each team landed:

- **Mercedes**: Got it right, and it isn't close on pace. The problem is the battery.
  Russell's PU let go while leading at Montreal, Antonelli's died four laps from home at
  Barcelona while running second, and at Spa an energy-deployment glitch cut Russell's
  power through Eau Rouge and pitched him into a race-ending lap-1 clash with Hamilton.
  James Allison said the first two failures "originate in the same broad part of the
  battery"; the fix is phasing new battery modules into the fleet over the season while
  dialling back performance demands, not an overnight cure. Wolff has been blunt about the
  cost — "we just can't compete for a championship if every second race a car is losing fat
  points." The bill came due at Monza: those failures had eaten through Antonelli's season
  allocation, so Mercedes took a 30-place grid penalty for fresh parts and he won from 19th
  anyway. The car is on top of the sport right now.
- **McLaren**: The upgrade package at Hungary turned their season — first win of the year
  straight after it, and a new rear wing and rear brake ducts at Zandvoort billed as
  completing it. Norris won Hungary and Zandvoort back to back, the Dutch one off a fiddly
  damp-to-dry race he rated "probably one of my better wins." Since then the car has been
  quick and the results haven't followed: Verstappen beat both of them to P3 at Monza in a
  straight-line-speed contest, and at Madrid Norris led on merit and lost it to VSC timing
  he had no say in. Piastri is the bigger worry — P5 at Monza, P8 at Madrid, and 66 points
  behind his team-mate. Stella's diagnosis: "I don't think it has anything to do with any
  psychological kind of opportunity — this is purely technical." The 2026 car has so little
  grip that Piastri "needs to adapt the way he drives, the way he thinks, and his
  expectations", and the thinking costs him tenths. It shows up on Sundays, not Saturdays —
  0.068s off Norris in qualifying on average, 0.227s in races.
- **Red Bull-Ford**: The in-house PU is a work in progress, trending up. A big Austria
  upgrade package put Verstappen on the podium there, but the story has been the
  "Macarena" revolving rear wing — a trick Ferrari pioneered and Red Bull adapted, run
  since Miami. It failed to close properly twice, dumping rear downforce mid-corner and
  binning Verstappen in Austria qualifying and again at Stowe while he ran P3 four laps
  from the end at Silverstone. "It's getting very dangerous for me," he said; the team
  parked the wing for Spa and went back to its old conventional spec. It could return once
  modified, but it hasn't yet. P3 at Monza and P2 at Madrid look like progress, but don't
  read them as the PU deficit closing. Red Bull's real gap is energy deployment, which the
  FIA's ADUO ranking doesn't measure at all: it scores the combustion side only, has Red
  Bull as the benchmark, and therefore locks them out of upgrading the electrical side they
  actually need. Verstappen at Monza: "we just didn't have the same kind of deployment as
  Mercedes and it was quite clear when I got into the lead." And drily, on being told again
  that he has the best engine: "apparently the fastest engine."
- **Audi**: First season with its own PU, and reliability still bites. Hulkenberg's car
  caught fire on the reconnaissance lap to the Miami sprint grid; Allan McNish admitted
  the team thought they'd fixed it in the garage and clearly hadn't. They're also the team
  currently picking a fight with the stewards — see the Tsunoda restart appeal under Monza.
- **Cadillac**: The new 11th team, still backmarkers but operating better every weekend.
  Miami was their best showing — Perez beat Albon's Williams and Stroll's Aston in the
  sprint, both cars finished. Monaco was the gut-punch: Perez crossed the line in the
  points, which would have been Cadillac's maiden F1 point, then copped a 10-second
  post-race penalty for being out of position at the restart and dropped to last.

### "Super clipping" — the buzzword of 2026

With the new ~50/50 ICE/electric split, cars run out of battery before the end of long
straights and visibly slow — sometimes by 50 km/h — while drivers are still flat on the
throttle. Norris said the cars are "probably the worst ever made." Hamilton called it
"the least enjoyable part" of the new rules. Suzuka's 130R was reportedly the worst spot
for it. Alonso went nuclear at Monaco — "probably the worst generation of cars I ever
drove," and flatly, "hybrid cars should not be racing. It's as simple as that."

### Bearman's 50G crash and the rule tweaks it triggered

Ollie Bearman crashed at Suzuka's Spoon at 191 mph after closing on Colapinto's Alpine
roughly 50 km/h faster — a textbook super-clipping speed delta. He walked away with a
bruised knee, but the crash forced an FIA-stakeholder meeting on April 20 that produced
mid-season "refinements" landing at Miami: qualifying harvest cut from 8 MJ to 7 MJ
(lower still on 12 specific circuits), MGU-K deployment capped at 250 kW outside "key
acceleration zones" (350 kW inside them), race boost mode capped at +150 kW, a new
"low-power start" detection that auto-deploys MGU-K if a car has a weak getaway, and
higher tyre-blanket temperatures for inters. Verstappen called the changes "a tickle."
Monaco then got its own one-off treatment — a mandatory "Rev1" engine map plus a total
ban on active-aero "straight mode" for the weekend — the first circuit-specific PU mode
ever mandated.

The bigger lever landed around Barcelona: F1 agreed to shift the ICE/electric balance
away from 50/50 — to 58/42 in 2027 and 60/40 in 2028. That 60/40 is the exact ratio
Verstappen had named as his minimum to stay in the sport, and he cautiously approved:
"the changes they are making are heading into the right direction." The super-clipping
era has a sunset date now, even if the 2026 cars are stuck with it.

### Antonelli's breakout — and then Monza

Kimi Antonelli became the youngest driver ever to lead the F1 World Championship, at 19,
and he has spent the season turning that into a cushion. Eight wins now, the latest of
them a Madrid race Norris had in hand until the VSC fell wrong — Kimi's own verdict was
"it's good to win, but it doesn't feel as good because I think Lando today deserved to
win," which tells you
something about him too. The only things that have dented him all year have been his own
car and the stewards, not a rival on track: the Barcelona PU failure four laps from the
flag while running second, and a bizarre left-front wheel-shield failure at Silverstone
that turned a near-certain win into 16th.

Monza is the one people will remember. Now 20, he arrived at his home race carrying a
30-place grid penalty for three fresh power-unit elements, lined up 19th on hards, gambled
on mediums at the restart and was leading inside 18 laps. Brundle called it one of the best
races he'd commentated on in 30 years. He's the first Italian to win at Monza since
Ludovico Scarfiotti in 1966 — and he did it in a Mercedes, which the tifosi seemed
entirely fine with once the noise started.

The clearest sign of where he stands isn't any single result: Mercedes ordered Russell to
move over for him at Zandvoort, then split the strategy his way at Monza (see Rivalries).
That team has picked its horse, and right now nobody is laying a glove on him.

### Verstappen re-signed — and what's left of the driver market

He's staying. Red Bull announced on the Thursday of the Dutch GP weekend (August 20) that
Verstappen has extended through the end of 2030, when he'll be 33 — the story that
dominated half a season, resolved in a press release. Worth knowing: the performance exit
clause survived into the new deal (Max: "I've never disclosed anything in my contract, so
yeah, that stays the same"), and he's negotiated permission to race endurance events,
24-hour races included, when the F1 calendar allows. His race engineer of a decade,
Gianpiero Lambiase, is still leaving for McLaren, but in 2028.

Sainz re-signed with Williams on multi-year terms the day before, completing their line-up
alongside Albon. That leaves McLaren, Ferrari, Williams and Cadillac as the only teams with
both 2027 seats confirmed. Still open: Red Bull's second seat, both Racing Bulls, and one
apiece at Aston Martin, Haas and Alpine — with Mercedes' and Audi's line-ups yet to be made
official.

That Red Bull seat is the one to watch. German reports have the team preparing an extension
for Hadjar as a reward for his season — awkward to be running while he's three races into a
broken-wrist layoff and Liam Lawson, the man he replaced, is scoring in the car instead.
The other pressure is from below: Red Bull junior Nikola Tsolov leads F2 for Campos — he
took the lead at Silverstone by becoming the first driver in the category's history to win
three races in a row — and has since done his first F1 test, 690km in a Racing Bulls car at
Imola. Red Bull has to decide whether to accelerate him or leave well alone.

### Ferrari's year two — and the rules-of-engagement row

Ferrari's home weekend detonated. On lap 1 at Monza Hamilton dived up the inside of
Leclerc at the first chicane, they touched, and Hamilton bounced through the gravel from
P4 to P10. Lap 2, Leclerc — busy holding off Piastri — lost the rear on the exit of
Parabolica and went into the tyre barrier at 60G. Nobody was penalised (stewards: racing
incident) but the pundits split down the middle, Rosberg blaming Leclerc for not leaving
room and Brundle reckoning Leclerc was far enough ahead to be entitled to the line.
Leclerc took it on the chin: "We went too far. I tried to do the tightest corner possible,
but that was just not enough." Hamilton went further
and made it about the team: "When I was at Mercedes, we had written rules of engagement...
Here, there are no rules. We need to address that." And then the sting — he thinks it's
already moot: "we just lost so much ground to them on points, and it's far too late for
those decisions to come." Vasseur, who'd argued before Monza that it was too early to pick
a driver (citing McLaren's 2025 experience with Piastri), has now shifted: "for sure, at
one stage you have to make a decision... Now we have time to discuss with the guys."
Madrid didn't force the issue — Hamilton's brakes failed on lap 6 and Leclerc ran fourth
alone — so the question is still sitting there unanswered.

The backdrop: year two at Maranello delivered the moment the whole move was for, Hamilton's
maiden Ferrari win at Barcelona, plus the first all-British podium (Hamilton, Russell,
Norris) since 1968. Leclerc answered with a controlled Silverstone win, his first there.
The pecking order has changed hands all year without either landing a decisive blow, which
is precisely why the no-rules question got so loud at Monza.

### The Madring debuted, and nobody could pass

Madrid's new circuit — a hybrid of public roads and purpose-built track, Miami-style,
wrapped around the IFEMA exhibition centre in Barajas — got a rough first verdict. The
layout took the blame: four on-track overtakes after lap one, against ten at Monaco this
year and a season average of 61. Ocon's read on why was a total lack of tyre degradation
and graining, so nobody's pace fell away and nobody got a look in. Albon had called it
before the race: "You just put the car in the middle of the track and you're not going to
get a car that's going to go past you." Verstappen's complaint is the braking zones: too
many of them make you brake and turn at once, which leaves "only one line and that makes it
very easy to defend as well." He also said he still needed "to figure out a corner where to
pass," and offered, only half-joking, "if you give me the track map, I'll sort it out."
Norris wants Turns 18-19 gone ("useless"). The
junior categories had the opposite problem: F3's pre-event test here racked up 19 red flags
across two days, 11 of them crashes, and the weekend itself produced a fiery F2 shunt and a
nine-car F3 pile-up at Turn 7 on the opening lap of race two. F1 practice took out Lindblad,
Hamilton and Bearman, whose Haas couldn't be repaired after FP3. The circuit already has
three changes on the table for 2027 — widening the approach to the Turn 5/6 chicane,
tightening Turn 13 after La Monumental to create a braking zone off the tow, and widening
and reprofiling Turn 20 — to be worked through with the FIA, the drivers and the teams over
the coming weeks. Sainz's steer: "good street tracks like Baku, Macau tend to have one
single overtaking spot with a very long straight."

### The VSC rule Norris wants changed

The Madrid race turned on a technicality. Stroll's Aston stopped with brake trouble at
Turn 20, Norris — leading and comfortable — was just past the pit entry when the VSC came
out, and everyone behind him pitted at a fraction of the usual cost. He came in the next
lap, by which point the VSC had ended, and a botched front-right change left him stationary
for seven seconds on top of it. Around 20 seconds lost relative to the field, and a
near-certain win became third. "I was just unlucky, I was the only one on the whole grid who
doesn't get a pitstop. So I lose 20 seconds on the whole grid, this is out of my control,
it's out of their control... it's a rule that shouldn't define a race," he said, and: "A
race should be won by the guy who can put in the best performance... it's just unfair." His
two suggested fixes, both already raised in drivers' briefings: leave the VSC out for at
least a full lap so everyone gets the same chance to stop, or close the pit entry under VSC
altogether. Stella defended coming in — "we considered staying out but you have to accept
the fact that now you're on relatively used medium tyres and the people behind you will be
on new hards, so you're never going to open a gap" — while saying he was puzzled about what
the right answer is and that it belongs in front of the Strategy Advisory Committee. Zak
Brown, less diplomatically: "That was our race by 20 or 25 seconds. He was dominant, he was
gone."

### Two things worth remembering about this calendar

Bahrain and Saudi Arabia were both cancelled — a first for the modern calendar,
geopolitical tensions forcing the FIA's hand — leaving a five-week gap between Suzuka and
Miami. And active aero arrived: teams adjusting aero surfaces on the fly, DRS on steroids
but with defensive and cornering uses too. It has changed overtaking dynamics a lot, and
some drivers adapted faster than others.

## Rivalries to Watch

- **Antonelli vs Russell** (Mercedes): Not a rivalry between equals any more — it's a
  number one and a driver who keeps being asked to make way, politely. Two weekends
  running now, Mercedes' calls have gone Antonelli's way. Zandvoort was explicit: Russell
  had arrived saying team orders were "not even a consideration at the moment," won the
  Sprint, then was told with six laps to go to let Antonelli by after the team pitted Kimi
  onto fresh tyres under a late VSC. His radio was pure acid — "Is he fighting for the win?
  Or, is he planning to catch Norris?" (Norris was over 11 seconds up the road) — and then he
  U-turned once he'd cooled off, calling it the right decision. Monza did it again by
  stealth: under the lap-28 VSC, Antonelli and Verstappen pitted while Russell stayed out,
  believing both Mercedes were. He lost the place late to a car on far fresher tyres, and
  Brundle's read was that he "will feel bruised by how that played out." Madrid needed no
  orders at all — Russell tangled with Piastri on lap 1 and spent the day out of the
  picture in fifth while his team-mate won. Earlier flashpoints: the Canada sprint contact
  and Wolff's live "concentrate on the driving, not on the radio moaning" telling-off, and
  Barcelona, where Mercedes conceded it "might have cost itself" the win by letting them
  scrap freely. Nobody at Brackley is letting them scrap freely now.
- **Norris vs Piastri** (McLaren): Reigning champion vs the Australian who almost took
  the 2025 title, and the "papaya rules" détente is fraying. Hungary was the blow-up:
  Piastri led the first half, McLaren's second-stop call to cover a Hamilton undercut
  dropped him into traffic behind Sainz ("Get out of the f****** way you idiot"), Norris
  overcut into the lead in clear air, and Piastri went full ice — "nice of you guys to
  factor that one in, thanks," then "Don't f****** talk to me" — before a gearbox failure
  ended his day. Since then Zandvoort went Norris's way again (win vs P6), and at Monza
  they spent the closing laps swapping P4 — Piastri covering the inside at Curva Grande and
  putting Norris two wheels on the grass, Norris on the radio claiming he'd been pushed off,
  then taking the place at Ascari on the last lap. Brundle's word for how it looked was
  "personal." Both walked it back afterwards and Stella called it "much more benign than
  what it might have looked like in the heat of the moment" — but McLaren said it would
  review the move anyway. Madrid kept them apart: Norris led on merit before the VSC, Piastri
  was hit twice on lap 1 — the first by Russell — broke a front wing endplate and salvaged
  eighth with McLaren only able to re-angle the wing at his stop rather than change it. The
  gap between them is 66 points now, and the interesting question is how long Piastri stays
  civil about it.
- **Hamilton vs Leclerc** (Ferrari): No longer just a pecking-order question — they
  collided on lap 1 at Monza, Leclerc crashed out on lap 2, and Hamilton came out of it
  demanding Ferrari write down rules of engagement it doesn't have. See "Ferrari's year
  two" above. Madrid gave the argument a rest by force: Hamilton was running third — handed
  back by Verstappen after the Red Bull cut Turn 1 — when his brakes went, and Leclerc
  quietly brought it home fourth.
- **Norris vs Verstappen**: 2025 title rivals, now in very different tiers of machinery.
  Can Verstappen do it with an inferior car like 2024? The answer this year is no. The
  team-mates-in-2027 subplot is dead — Max re-signed with Red Bull to 2030 — so this
  stays what it's been all season: occasional flashes when their races intersect,
  nothing sustained.

## Memes & Fan Culture

- Russell remains a meme magnet. His expressive radio reactions and PowerPoint-era
  greatest hits are still circulating, now joined by his "watching my teammate take my
  pole" face from Suzuka.
- **The Antonelli–Bono prank war.** Mercedes have leaned all the way in, posting videos of
  Kimi sabotaging Peter Bonnington (Hamilton's old engineer, now Antonelli's). The one
  where Kimi gets the team to deactivate Bono's access card across Brackley HQ — Bono
  hitting a red-flashing door muttering "the little s\*\*\* has done it again" — went
  everywhere. The designated wholesome beat of a season full of regulation arguments.
- **"All on me"** — Leclerc's radio after binning his Miami podium on the final lap, in
  the same lineage as the classic "Inters?" and "It's like driving on ice."
- **"Concentrate on the driving, not on the radio moaning"** — Wolff to Antonelli
  mid-sprint at Montreal. Instant T-shirt material.
- **"Don't f****** talk to me"** — Piastri's ice-cold radio to his own McLaren pit wall
  at Hungary, paired with the earlier "Get out of the f****** way you idiot" at Sainz.
- **"Is he fighting for the win? Or, is he planning to catch Norris?"** — Russell's
  withering radio at Zandvoort on being told to let his own title rival past. Deadpan,
  devastating, and the fact he then publicly agreed with the call afterwards only made it
  better.
- **Zandvoort's orange goodbye.** Verstappen's Delft Blue helmet and orange-shouldered
  suit for his last home race, a record 325,000 across the weekend — and then he stuck it
  in the wall at the last corner of lap one, in front of every one of them.
- **"There are no rules"** — Hamilton on Ferrari's absent rules of engagement after the
  Monza clash, delivered with the weary precision of a man who has had this argument
  before. Immediately weaponised by both halves of the Ferrari fanbase against each other.
- **Monza's Italian winner.** A home driver winning at Monza for the first time in 60
  years, from 19th, in front of a grandstand that turned up to watch Ferrari — and Ferrari
  had one car through the gravel and the other in the barrier before lap 3. The
  tifosi-cheering-a-Mercedes footage is the fan-edit fuel of the season.
- **"I almost fell asleep as well"** — Bortoleto, asked whether the Madring was as dull to

  four overtakes.
- **"You don't expect to go around the outside of a champion"** — Hamilton's dry
  comeback after Verstappen battled him hard in Austria and barked "Clear penalty!" over
  the radio (stewards: no action). Two old rivals trading needle a decade on.
- **"Maybe I've hit a black cat"** — Verstappen gallows-humouring Red Bull's run of
  rotten luck: "I don't know what's causing it."
- **Hamilton's first Ferrari win** was the season's big feel-good moment — an emotional
  maiden victory for the Scuderia and that first all-British podium since 1968, a rare
  break from the regulation-grumbling discourse.

## Recent Incidents & Controversies

### Spanish GP (Round 14, Madrid, September 11-13)

- **Norris took pole** by 0.011s from Antonelli — 1:31.824 to 1:31.835 — and led the race
  until the mid-race VSC for Stroll's stopped Aston caught him past the pit entry. Antonelli
  and Verstappen pitted cheaply and came out ahead; Norris came in the next lap, after the
  VSC had ended, and a seven-second front-right change finished the job. Antonelli by 4.351s
  from Verstappen, Norris a further 0.7s back. See "The VSC rule Norris wants changed."
- **Brakes everywhere.** Hamilton was running third, handed the place back by Verstappen
  after the Red Bull cut Turn 1, when the pedal "took a step" into Turn 5 and he carried too
  much speed in and brushed the barrier; it came back, then went fully long a few corners
  later and he retired inside seven laps. Vasseur: "we tried to fix it from the pit wall and
  then it happened again a couple of corners later." Stroll went out with the same
  complaint, triggering the decisive VSC. Perez also failed to finish, with a suspected
  water-pressure problem.
- **Sainz's home weekend fell apart.** A three-place grid penalty for impeding Bottas in
  Q1 — Williams never warned him the Cadillac was on a lap — then a lap-one mess: contact
  with Tsunoda, and squeezing Alonso into the wall at Turn 5, worth a five-second penalty.
  He served it and retired on lap 43 with the Tsunoda damage. Alonso limped to 17th, two
  laps down: "the engine was very bad today... the boost control was difficult. I nearly
  stalled on the pitstops."
- **The rest of the points:** Leclerc P4, Russell P5 after lap-1 contact with Piastri,
  Lawson P6 in his third Red Bull outing, Colapinto P7 in a genuinely good Alpine drive,
  Piastri P8 with front wing damage, then Lindblad beating Hulkenberg to P9 at the flag.
- **A dud of a race on a brand new track.** Four on-track overtakes after lap one, against
  ten at Monaco. Bortoleto: "I almost fell asleep as well." Norris: "I don't know how
  someone looks at this design and goes: 'Mint'." Details under "The Madring debuted."
- **Practice was carnage.** Lindblad into the barrier at Turn 13 in FP2; FP3 red-flagged
  twice, Hamilton head-on into the final corner and Bearman into the wall at Turn 10 in the
  dying seconds. Haas had two hours and couldn't repair the chassis damage, so Bearman sat
  out qualifying entirely.
- **Hadjar's third race out.** The wrist — a small crack in the bone, from a boxing workout
  over the break — has now cost him Zandvoort, Monza and Madrid, with Lawson in the Red Bull
  and Tsunoda back at Racing Bulls throughout. Mekies says the recovery is on schedule and
  within expectations, but Red Bull won't put him back in short of full fitness — he was in
  Madrid wearing a brace. Another assessment before Baku, which is the target for his
  comeback. Lawson is having comfortably his best season in F1
  ("I'm the happiest I have been in F1") in the seat he was dropped from after two races in
  2025 — and P6 here won't hurt his case for keeping it.

### Italian GP (Round 13, Monza, September 4-6)

- **Antonelli wins from 19th.** Mercedes banked a 30-place grid hit for three fresh
  power-unit elements at his home race. Wolff: "Our calculations say that that's the best
  track to take it. Obviously, algorithms don't take their nationality into
  consideration... we're here to fight for a championship and not get the most PR."
  Started 19th on hards, 12th by the red flag, mediums for the restart when most went hard,
  into the lead inside 18 laps, home by 3.857s — helped by a lap-28 VSC where he and
  Verstappen pitted and Russell, who believed both Mercedes were staying out, didn't.
- **Ferrari's home race lasted two laps.** Hamilton went inside Leclerc at the first
  chicane on lap 1, contact, Hamilton through the gravel and down to 10th. Lap 2, Leclerc
  — holding off Piastri — put his left-rear on the green-painted strip that contains a
  drain cover on the exit of Parabolica, lost the rear, and hit the tyre barrier at 60G.
  He's fine. Red flag, standing restart. Stewards called the lap-1 clash a racing incident;
  the team-orders row it set off is under "Ferrari's year two."
- **Verstappen P3, ahead of both McLarens** on a pure power-and-drag circuit — though the
  analysis afterwards was that Red Bull's real deficit at Monza was energy deployment, not
  the combustion engine. Norris P4 and Piastri P5 after a scrappy but contact-free squabble
  settled at Ascari on the last lap. Hamilton recovered to P6.
- **Audi moves to appeal the Tsunoda non-penalty.** After Leclerc's crash, the restart
  needed an extra formation lap: Tsunoda drifted towards the P15 box before veering right
  into his own P14 slot, ending up angled towards the pit wall, and race director Rui
  Marques sent the field round again. The rules (Art. B5.9.4) say whoever causes an extra
  formation lap starts from the pit lane, but the stewards found no breach — the extra lap
  "resulted from the race director's assessment of the situation" rather than being
  directly caused by the driver, Racing Bulls showed his contact patch was inside the grid
  box, and Race Control never told the team he was the cause. Tsunoda kept P10 and the last
  point — which would otherwise have gone to Bortoleto, P11. Audi lodged its intention to
  appeal and has since confirmed it is going through with the full appeal rather than
  letting it drop; its case is that the rulebook is black and white and the notification
  question is irrelevant. If it succeeds, Tsunoda's penalty converts to 30 seconds and
  Bortoleto inherits the point. The FIA has yet to set a hearing date.
### Earlier in the season

- **Dutch GP** (Round 12, Zandvoort, August 21-23): Russell won the Sprint from lights to
  flag; the race was red-flagged after one lap when Verstappen dipped a wheel over the
  white line at the final corner and destroyed the car in front of his home crowd. Norris
  won from Antonelli and Russell, with Lawson P7 in his first weekend standing in for the
  injured Hadjar. The Mercedes swap — fresh tyres for Antonelli under a late VSC, a "let
  him past" call with six laps to go, a very sarcastic radio reply, a full retraction an
  hour later — is under Rivalries.
- **Hungarian GP** (Round 11, July 25-26): McLaren's first win of 2026 and an intra-team
  civil war. Piastri led the first half, lost it to a pit-priority call, raged on the radio
  and then retired with a gearbox failure; Norris overcut into the lead in clear air.
  Verstappen P2 in a race he called one of "survival."
- **Belgian GP** (Round 10, July 19): Antonelli pole to flag, Verstappen P3 on the reverted
  wing. Russell lost battery deployment through Eau Rouge, fell into the pack, collided
  with Hamilton at Les Combes and retired — Wolff owned the root cause (see Mercedes
  above). Colapinto's double pass at Les Combes was the move of the day.
- **FIA ADUO ruling backfires on Red Bull** (still contested): The "Adjustment of
  Underperforming Operations" provision allows limited PU upgrades for struggling
  manufacturers. Honda, Ferrari and Audi were cleared — and the FIA's first ranking
  judged Red Bull-Ford to have the BEST engine of 2026, meaning Red Bull is the one team
  NOT allowed to upgrade. Verstappen was baffled: "We just feel surprised, because we
  don't feel like we are the best." Red Bull contested it; the FIA is reviewing.
- **British GP** (Round 9, July 4-5): Leclerc got ahead off the line and never looked
  back. Antonelli took his maiden F1 sprint win on Saturday, then a wheel-shield failure
  wrecked his Sunday; Verstappen's second rear-wing failure in two weekends put him in
  the Stowe gravel with four laps left.
- **Austrian GP** (Round 8, June 26-28): Russell's "cold-blooded" win nursing a failed
  drinks system in brutal heat, Verstappen P5 to P2 on Red Bull's big upgrade. Racing
  Bulls rookie Arvid Lindblad ignored orders to hold station behind Lawson ("we are not
  fighting") and passed him anyway — Lawson audibly furious, "I lift for brakes and then
  get attacked." Pundits have been touting Lindblad as Red Bull's "next Verstappen" since.
- **Spanish GP** (Round 7, Barcelona, June 12-14): Hamilton's maiden Ferrari win;
  Antonelli's PU failure four laps from home while running second; Leclerc's
  power-steering DNF.
- **Monaco GP** (Round 6, June 7): Antonelli lights to flag from pole, carnage behind —
  seven DNFs including Verstappen on lap 1. Hadjar took P3, his first podium in Red Bull
  colours. Russell turned a likely podium into P12 by himself, a 0.1 km/h pit-speed
  breach compounded by a botched penalty service: "I probably gained one tenth in the
  whole pit lane and lost 13 positions."
- **Canadian GP** (Round 5, May 22-24): Russell's PU let go on lap 30 while he led. He
  sat stunned, smacked the car and hurled his headrest onto the track — "it feels like
  somebody doesn't want me to fight for this championship" — drawing an FIA "unsafe act"
  investigation and a suspended €5,000 fine. Hamilton P2, Verstappen's first podium of 2026.
- **Miami GP** (Round 4, May 2-3): Chaos. Hulkenberg's Audi caught fire on the
  reconnaissance lap to the sprint grid; Gasly flipped after contact with Lawson. Antonelli
  won. Leclerc threw away podium pace into the wall on the final lap, then took a 20s
  penalty for repeatedly using the run-offs.
