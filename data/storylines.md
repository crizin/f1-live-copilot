# 2026 Season Storylines

> Things Claude might not know from training data — recent drama, memes, narratives.
> Updated throughout the season as stories develop.
> Championship points live in `standings.md`. This file carries the narrative, not the table.

Last update: 2026-09-21, in the gap after the Spanish GP (Round 14, Madrid, September
11-13 — the Madring's first F1 race). Next up is the season's first triple header,
starting with Azerbaijan. The Baku weekend runs a day early — FP1/FP2 Thursday, FP3 and
qualifying Friday, and the 51-lap race on **Saturday September 26**, because Azerbaijan's
Remembrance Day falls on the Sunday. First Saturday Grand Prix Baku has held.

**Where it stands:** Antonelli's championship lead is now measured in race wins rather than
points, and Russell — his own team-mate — is the closest thing to a challenger left. Eight
wins, the last two back-to-back: from 19th on the grid at Monza, then a Madrid race he
openly said Norris deserved to win, handed to him by VSC timing. Ferrari's month has been
miserable — a two-lap home race at Monza, then brake failure that ended Hamilton's Madrid
after seven laps — with the rules-of-engagement row he started still unresolved, Vasseur
having publicly rejected the demand for written rules. Madrid's new circuit got dunked on
harder than the race got discussed, and is in line for layout changes next year. The 2027
driver market is largely settled — Sainz to Williams, Verstappen to Red Bull through 2030 —
with Red Bull's second seat the big one still open, and Hadjar, three weekends out with a
broken wrist and hoping to return at Baku, expected to be kept on anyway.

## The Big Stories

### Zandvoort's last dance — and it ended in the wall

The Dutch Grand Prix is gone from the calendar. The promoter chose not to continue past
2026, citing the long-term financial risk of hosting without state subsidies rather than
any lack of demand; nobody has ruled out a return, but there's no deal. A record 325,000
came across the three days — and Verstappen put it in the barrier at the last corner of lap
one in front of every one of them.

### The new rules reshuffled the grid

The 2026 power unit overhaul — electric power tripled, MGU-H gone, 100% sustainable fuel
— redrew the order. Where each team landed:

- **Mercedes**: Got it right, and it isn't close on pace. Antonelli has eight wins.
  The problem is the battery. Russell's PU let go while leading at Montreal, Antonelli's
  died four laps from home at Barcelona while running second, and at Spa an
  energy-deployment glitch cut Russell's power through Eau Rouge and pitched him into a
  race-ending lap-1 clash with Hamilton. James Allison said the first two failures
  "originate in the same broad part of the battery"; the fix is phasing new modules into
  the fleet over the season, not an overnight cure. Wolff on the cost — "we just can't
  compete for a championship if every second race a car is losing fat points" — and on Spa:
  "it 100% goes on us." Raw pace was never the issue. The bill came due at Monza, where the
  failures had eaten Antonelli's allocation and cost him 30 grid places; he won anyway. The
  car is on top of the sport right now.
- **McLaren**: The upgrade package at Hungary turned their season — first win of the year
  straight after it, and a new rear wing and rear brake ducts at Zandvoort billed as
  completing it. Norris won Hungary and Zandvoort back to back, the Dutch one off a fiddly
  damp-to-dry race he rated "probably one of my better wins." Monza was the reality check:
  Verstappen held both of them off for P3 in a straight-line-speed contest, and the two
  papaya cars spent the last five laps trading P4 until Norris made it stick at Ascari on
  the final lap — no contact, but close enough that McLaren said it would review it, after
  Piastri's defence at Curva Grande put Norris two wheels on the grass. Madrid was crueller:
  pole, race controlled, then lost to the pit wall's luck and then its hands — past the pit
  entry when the VSC came out, then a 7s front-right. Piastri's gearbox failure from the
  lead fight in Hungary was a reminder the machinery can still bite too.
- **Red Bull-Ford**: The in-house PU is a work in progress, trending up. A big Austria
  upgrade package put Verstappen on the podium there, but the story has been the
  "Macarena" revolving rear wing — a trick Ferrari pioneered and Red Bull adapted, run
  since Miami. It failed to close properly twice, dumping rear downforce mid-corner and
  binning Verstappen in Austria qualifying and again at Stowe while he ran P3 four laps
  from the end at Silverstone. "It's getting very dangerous for me," he said; the team
  parked it from Spa and reverted to the old conventional spec while chasing a fix. He
  called the compromise "slower" but worth it, and it held. Monza gave him P3 with both
  McLarens behind — but don't read that as the PU deficit closing. What cost him there was
  energy deployment, which the FIA's ADUO ranking doesn't measure at all: it scores the
  combustion side only, has Red Bull as the benchmark, and therefore locks them out of
  upgrading the electrical side they actually need. Verstappen, drily, on being told again
  that he has the best engine: "apparently the fastest engine." Madrid gave him P2 anyway,
  on a track where he'd warned "there are no corners where you can pass."
- **Audi**: First season with its own PU, and reliability still bites — Hulkenberg's car
  caught fire on the reconnaissance lap to the Miami sprint grid. They're also the team
  currently picking a fight with the stewards over one point — see the Tsunoda appeal
  under Monza.
- **Cadillac**: The new 11th team, still backmarkers but operating better every weekend.
  Monaco was the gut-punch: Perez crossed the line in the points, which would have been
  Cadillac's maiden F1 point, then copped a 10-second post-race penalty for being out of
  position at the restart and dropped to last.

### The Madring's debut went badly

F1's newest circuit — a part-street, part-purpose-built lap around the IFEMA exhibition
grounds in Madrid — arrived with a lot of noise and produced four on-track overtakes after
lap one. For scale, Monza managed 78 and Miami 88. The drivers did not spare it. Bortoleto:
"I almost fell asleep." Norris: "I don't know how someone looks at this design and goes,
mint!" Russell's complaint was structural — the longest straight dumps you into a tight
roundabout, so there's nothing to set up a move with. Sainz called some of the blind corners
unsafe and put them in the Jeddah/Baku category. Alonso's verdict on La Monumental, the
banked showpiece: "the driver input is just nothing."

The fixes being asked for are specific rather than vague: Norris wants Turns 18-19 deleted
for a longer run into a wider hairpin, Albon wants the Turn 5 chicane redone after watching
F2 and F3 go side-by-side through it, and Bottas's problem is how blind sectors two and
three are — "especially people on slow laps" — with Saudi Arabia's later revisions as the
precedent for fixing it. Even the winner wants surgery — Antonelli's line was that "there
are some corners that need to be changed to create more overtaking opportunities." The other
target is the pit lane — enormously long and split into two sections, which Norris reckons
kills off any pit strategy worth watching. It even went political: Spain's
transport minister called the race "an absolute dud" with "a little train of cars from start
to finish."

Changes are expected for next year, and the organisers have said on the record they're open
to them. Event director Luis García Abad says the FIA "defined clearly which is the way to
improve the track layout, and we will be open because we will have to reinstall the track
for the next year" — the circuit has to be put back up for the next race anyway, so the
layout work can ride along with that. Madrid is on a 10-year deal, and this was race one
of it.

### "Super clipping" — the buzzword of 2026

With the new ~50/50 ICE/electric split, cars run out of battery before the end of long
straights and visibly slow — sometimes by 50 km/h — while drivers are still flat on the
throttle. Norris said the cars are "probably the worst ever made." Hamilton called it
"the least enjoyable part" of the new rules. Suzuka's 130R was reportedly the worst spot
for it. At Monaco, a track with barely any energy-saving pressure, Alonso went nuclear
anyway — "probably the worst generation of cars I ever drove," and flatly, "hybrid cars
should not be racing." His gripe there was the unpredictable engine braking: with a full
battery the car gives none, only forward push.

### Bearman's 50G crash and the rule tweaks it triggered

Ollie Bearman crashed at Suzuka's Spoon at 191 mph after closing on Colapinto's Alpine
roughly 50 km/h faster — a textbook super-clipping speed delta. He walked away with a
bruised knee, but the crash forced an FIA-stakeholder meeting that produced mid-season
"refinements" landing at Miami: qualifying harvest cut from 8 MJ to 7 MJ, MGU-K deployment
capped at 250 kW outside "key acceleration zones," race boost capped at +150 kW, and
auto-deploy detection for weak getaways. Verstappen called the changes "a tickle." Monaco
then got a one-off mandatory engine map plus a ban on active-aero "straight mode" — the
first circuit-specific PU mode ever mandated.

The bigger lever landed around Barcelona: F1 agreed to shift the ICE/electric balance
away from 50/50 — to 58/42 in 2027 and 60/40 in 2028. That 60/40 is the exact ratio
Verstappen had named as his minimum to stay in the sport, and he cautiously approved:
"the changes they are making are heading into the right direction." The super-clipping
era has a sunset date now, even if the 2026 cars are stuck with it.

### Antonelli's breakout — Monza, then Madrid

Kimi Antonelli became the youngest driver ever to lead the F1 World Championship, at 19,
and he has spent the season turning that into a cushion. Eight wins now, the last two
back-to-back. The only things that have dented him all year have been his own car and the
stewards, not a rival on track: the Barcelona PU failure four laps from the flag while
running second, and a bizarre left-front wheel-shield failure at Silverstone that turned a
near-certain win into 16th.

Monza is the one people will remember. Now 20, he took a 30-place PU penalty at his home
race, started 19th on hards, gambled on mediums at the lap-2 restart while most went hard,
and led inside 18 laps. Brundle called it one of the best races he'd commentated on in 30
years. First Italian to win at Monza since Ludovico Scarfiotti in 1966 — in a Mercedes,
which the tifosi seemed entirely fine with once the noise started.

Madrid a week later was the opposite kind of win — inherited rather than carved out — and
Antonelli said so unprompted: "It's good to win, but it doesn't feel as good because I
think Lando today deserved to win." Which is its own tell about the season. He isn't
having to beat anyone.

The clearest sign of where he stands isn't any single result: Mercedes ordered Russell to
move over for him at Zandvoort, then split the strategy his way at Monza (see Rivalries).
That team has picked its horse, and right now nobody is laying a glove on him.

### Verstappen re-signed — the saga is over

He's staying, through the end of 2030, announced on the Thursday of the Dutch GP weekend.
The story that dominated half a season — the performance exit clause he'd activated by
finishing the first half sixth, the open talk of retiring because he wasn't enjoying the
2026 cars, the real talks his management held with McLaren — resolved in a press release.
Two details still matter: the exit clause survives into the new deal ("that stays the
same"), and he's negotiated permission to race endurance events, 24-hour races included.
His race engineer of a decade, Gianpiero Lambiase, still leaves for McLaren — but in 2028.

Carlos Sainz re-signed with Williams on multi-year terms the day before, despite a
miserable 2026 in an FW48 that has him nowhere. That leaves McLaren, Ferrari, Williams and
Cadillac as the only teams with both 2027 seats confirmed. Still open: Red Bull's second
seat, both Racing Bulls, and one apiece at Aston Martin, Haas and Alpine — with Mercedes'
and Audi's line-ups yet to be made official.

On that Red Bull seat: German reports have the team preparing a contract extension for
Hadjar as a reward for his season — he's on a deal reviewed annually — and Mekies has been
hinting publicly in the same direction, which is notable given how short the tenure has been
for everyone else who has sat in that car. Awkward to keep running while Hadjar sits out
injured and Liam Lawson, the man he replaced, scores in the car instead. Whether Hadjar is
fit for Baku is a live question; see the Hadjar item under Monza.

### Ferrari's year two — and the rules-of-engagement row

Ferrari's home weekend detonated: Hamilton and Leclerc touched at the first chicane on lap
1, Leclerc crashed out of the race on lap 2, and Ferrari was down to one car in front of
the tifosi before anyone had settled. Nobody was penalised (stewards: racing incident) and
the pundits split down the middle. Leclerc took it on the chin: "We went too far." Hamilton
made it about the team: "When I was at Mercedes, we had written rules of engagement...
Here, there are no rules. We need to address that." And then the sting — he thinks it's
already moot: "we just lost so much ground to them on points, and it's far too late for
those decisions to come."

Where it's landed: nowhere. Hamilton and Vasseur met between Monza and Madrid — Hamilton
called it positive, with "different opinions" — and Vasseur has publicly pushed back. His
argument is that rules of engagement already exist in some form ("to say there are no rules
of engagement does not reflect reality") and that "you cannot expect every possible scenario
you might encounter on track to be written into the contracts, because inevitably everything
would become a matter of interpretation." By Madrid Thursday Hamilton said it hadn't come up
again. The pressure on Vasseur to just pick a driver isn't going away with it — though
Madrid made the argument academic for a weekend, Hamilton's brakes failing after seven laps
while Leclerc gambled a 48-lap opening stint on a safety car that never came, for fourth.

The backdrop: year two at Maranello delivered the moment the whole move was for, Hamilton's
maiden Ferrari win at Barcelona, and Leclerc answered with a controlled Silverstone win.
The pecking order has changed hands all year without either of them landing a decisive
blow, which is precisely why the no-rules question got so loud at Monza.

### Two things worth remembering about this calendar

Bahrain and Saudi Arabia were both cancelled — a first for the modern calendar,
geopolitical tensions forcing the FIA's hand — leaving a five-week gap between Suzuka and
Miami. And active aero arrived: teams adjusting aero surfaces on the fly, DRS on steroids
but with defensive and cornering uses too. It has changed overtaking dynamics a lot, and
some drivers adapted faster than others.

## Rivalries to Watch

- **Antonelli vs Russell** (Mercedes): Not a rivalry between equals any more — it's a
  number one and a driver who keeps being asked to make way, politely. Zandvoort was
  explicit: Russell won the Sprint, then was told with six laps to go to let Antonelli by
  after the team pitted Kimi onto fresh tyres under a late VSC. His radio was pure acid —
  "Is he fighting for the win? Or, is he planning to catch Norris?" (Norris was 11 seconds
  up the road) — then he U-turned once he'd cooled off, calling it the right call and noting
  that inverting on split strategies is long-standing team policy. Monza did it again by
  stealth: under the lap-28 VSC, Antonelli and Verstappen pitted while Russell, who believed
  both Mercedes were staying out, stayed out and lost the place late to fresher tyres.
  Brundle reckoned he "will feel bruised by how that played out." Russell hasn't blown up
  publicly either time. Earlier flashpoints: the Canada sprint contact and Wolff's live
  "concentrate on the driving, not on the radio moaning," and Barcelona, where Mercedes
  conceded it "might have cost itself" the win by letting them scrap freely. Nobody at
  Brackley is letting them scrap freely now.
- **Norris vs Piastri** (McLaren): Reigning champion vs the Australian who almost took the
  2025 title, and the "papaya rules" détente is fraying — though it's lopsided right now:
  Norris is 66 points up on him, and Piastri hasn't been on the podium since Miami.
  Hungary was the blow-up: Piastri led the first half, a second-stop call to cover a
  Hamilton undercut dropped him into traffic behind Sainz ("Get out of the f****** way you
  idiot"), Norris overcut into the lead in clear air, and Piastri went full ice — "Don't
  f****** talk to me" — before a gearbox failure ended his day. At Monza they spent the
  closing laps swapping P4, Piastri covering the inside at Curva Grande and putting Norris
  two wheels on the grass before Norris took it back at Ascari on the last lap. Brundle's
  word for how it looked was "personal." Both walked it back afterwards and Stella called
  it "much more benign than what it might have looked like in the heat of the moment" — but
  McLaren said it would review the move anyway. That's the thing to watch.
- **Hamilton vs Leclerc** (Ferrari): No longer just a pecking-order question — they
  collided on lap 1 at Monza, Leclerc crashed out on lap 2, and Hamilton came out of it
  demanding Ferrari write down rules of engagement it doesn't have. See "Ferrari's year
  two" above.
- **Norris vs Verstappen**: 2025 title rivals, now in very different tiers of machinery.
  Can Verstappen do it with an inferior car like 2024? The answer this year is no. The
  team-mates-in-2027 subplot is dead — Max re-signed with Red Bull to 2030 — so this
  stays what it's been all season: occasional flashes when their races intersect,
  nothing sustained.
- **Hamilton vs Verstappen**: dormant for years, awake again. Austria gave us "Clear
  penalty!" on the radio and Hamilton's "you don't expect to go around the outside of a
  champion" reply; Madrid gave us an early scrap over corner-cutting that both of them took
  to the radio, Hamilton wanting the stewards involved and Verstappen insisting Hamilton
  started it. Neither is in a title fight. Both are entirely willing anyway.

  maiden victory for the Scuderia and the first all-British podium since 1968.

## Recent Incidents & Controversies

### Spanish GP (Round 14, Madrid, September 11-13)

- **The VSC that decided it.** Norris took pole and led. On lap 15 Stroll's Aston stopped
  with a brake failure, the VSC came out a moment after Norris had gone past the pit entry,
  and Antonelli, Verstappen and Russell all pitted for free. A slow front-right on Norris's
  eventual stop — 7s standing still — finished the job. Antonelli won by 4.351s from
  Verstappen, Norris third at 5.089s and visibly unimpressed with the universe.
- **Hamilton out after seven laps.** Brake failure, and by his account he could barely stop
  the car; Ferrari retired it. Stroll's Aston went the same way. Leclerc salvaged P4 off a
  48-lap opening stint gambling on a safety car that never arrived.
- **Alonso v Sainz, in front of a Spanish crowd.** Lap 13, Alonso going round the outside,
  saying he was squeezed towards the wall. Five-second penalty for Sainz. Two Spaniards
  colliding at Spain's new race was not the Madrid debut anyone scripted.
- **The rest of the points**: Russell P5, Lawson P6 in the Red Bull, Colapinto P7, Piastri
  P8 after a quiet afternoon, Lindblad P9 holding off Hulkenberg for the last two places.
- Hamilton and Verstappen scrapped early over who cut what, both on the radio about it (see
  Rivalries) — and the race produced four on-track overtakes after lap one, which is how the
  circuit rather than the result became the story (see "The Madring's debut went badly").

### Italian GP (Round 13, Monza, September 4-6)

- **Antonelli wins from 19th**, by 3.857s — see "Antonelli's breakout" above. Mercedes took
  the 30-place penalty here because the numbers said Monza was the cheapest place for it,
  Wolff being Wolff about it: "algorithms don't take their nationality into consideration...
  we're here to fight for a championship and not get the most PR." Russell P2, and the
  lap-28 VSC that split them is under Rivalries.
- **Ferrari's home race lasted two laps.** Hamilton went inside Leclerc at the first
  chicane on lap 1, contact, Hamilton through the gravel to 10th. Lap 2, Leclerc lost the
  rear on the exit of Parabolica and hit the barrier at 60G — he's fine, but it brought
  the red flag and a standing restart. Stewards: racing incident. The row it set off is
  under "Ferrari's year two."
- **Verstappen P3, ahead of both McLarens**, from sixth on the grid on a pure power-and-drag
  circuit — though the post-race analysis put Red Bull's real deficit there on energy
  deployment, not the combustion engine. Norris P4 and Piastri P5 after a scrappy,
  contact-free squabble settled at Ascari on the last lap. Hamilton recovered to P6.
- **Audi's appeal over Tsunoda is live.** After Leclerc's crash the restart needed an extra
  formation lap — Tsunoda realised he was heading for the wrong grid slot and changed
  direction late to get into the right one. The rules say whoever causes an extra formation
  lap starts from the pit lane; the stewards found no breach — he wasn't out of his grid
  slot, he didn't impact his competitors, and race control never formally told the team he'd
  caused the extra lap — so Tsunoda kept P10 and his first point of the year. Audi has now
  gone through with the appeal, arguing the rulebook is black and white regardless of the
  race director's discretion. The unserved stop-and-go would convert to a 30-second post-race
  penalty, dropping Tsunoda out of the points and promoting Bortoleto from P11 into them. No
  hearing date set. Bortoleto on the merits: it's "straightforward in the rules."
- **Hadjar out, and Baku is the decision point.** The wrist — a hairline fracture from a
  boxing workout over the break — has cost him Zandvoort, Monza and Madrid, with Lawson in
  the Red Bull and Tsunoda back at Racing Bulls throughout. Mekies says there's "nothing
  wrong with Isack's recovery," just that it's taking the normal time for the injury, and
  Red Bull is running him through a simulator session to judge whether he's ready. The bar
  is that he has to feel close to 100 per cent — "if he feels the recovery is not
  completed, we will stick to our approach to put his recovery first" — and Lawson reckons
  the call may not come until Wednesday or Thursday of the Baku week, which leaves him
  preparing for both outcomes.
  Awkward for everyone, because Lawson is having comfortably his best season in F1 ("I'm the
  happiest I have been in F1") in the seat he was dropped from after two races in 2025:
  three weekends deputising and points in two of them, P7 at Zandvoort and P6 at Madrid,
  with Mekies calling the stint "very strong."

### Earlier in the season

The ones still worth knowing about:

- **Dutch GP** (Round 12): Zandvoort's farewell. Verstappen speared into the barrier at the
  last corner of lap one on a damp track and brought out the red flag; Norris won by 11.5s
  from Antonelli and Russell, with five retirements and Lawson P7 on his first weekend back
  in the Red Bull. Russell won Saturday's Sprint and was then told to move over on Sunday —
  see Rivalries.
- **FIA ADUO ruling backfires on Red Bull** (still contested): the "Adjustment of
  Underperforming Operations" provision allows limited PU upgrades for struggling
  manufacturers. Honda, Ferrari and Audi were cleared — and the FIA's first ranking judged
  Red Bull-Ford to have the BEST engine of 2026, meaning Red Bull is the one team NOT
  allowed to upgrade. Verstappen: "We just feel surprised, because we don't feel like we
  are the best." Red Bull contested it; the FIA is reviewing.
- **Hungarian GP** (Round 11): McLaren's first win of 2026 and an intra-team civil war —
  Piastri led the first half, lost it to a pit-priority call, raged on the radio, then
  retired with a gearbox failure. Norris won; Verstappen P2 in a race of "survival."
- **Belgian GP** (Round 10): Antonelli pole to flag. Russell lost battery deployment
  through Eau Rouge, collided with Hamilton at Les Combes and retired.
- **British GP** (Round 9): Leclerc's first Silverstone win. Antonelli's maiden sprint win
  on Saturday, then a wheel-shield failure wrecked his Sunday; Verstappen's second rear-wing
  failure in two weekends put him in the Stowe gravel with four laps left.
- **Austrian GP** (Round 8): Russell's "cold-blooded" win nursing a failed drinks system in
  brutal heat. Rookie Lindblad ignored orders to hold station behind Lawson ("we are not
  fighting") and passed him anyway — "I lift for brakes and then get attacked" — which is
  why pundits keep calling him Red Bull's next Verstappen.
- **Spanish GP** (Round 7, Barcelona): Hamilton's maiden Ferrari win; Antonelli's PU
  failure four laps from home while running second.
- **Monaco GP** (Round 6): Antonelli lights to flag, seven DNFs behind him including
  Verstappen on lap 1. Hadjar took P3, his first podium in Red Bull colours. Russell binned
  a likely podium with a 0.1 km/h pit-speed breach and a botched penalty service.
- **Canadian GP** (Round 5): Russell's PU let go on lap 30 while he led. He smacked the car
  and hurled his headrest onto the track — "it feels like somebody doesn't want me to fight
  for this championship" — and collected a suspended €5,000 fine for it.
- **Miami GP** (Round 4): Chaos. Hulkenberg's Audi caught fire on the reconnaissance lap to
  the sprint grid; Gasly flipped after contact with Lawson; Antonelli won; Leclerc threw
  away podium pace into the wall on the final lap.
